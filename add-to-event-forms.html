Add to Event · Forms

Simplifying form flows to drive organiser service requests

Role
Sole product designer
Timeline
2023 – 2024
Disciplines
Research, UX, UI, prototyping, user testing, design systems, developer hand-off
Problem
Request forms, the start of every marketplace transaction, had big unmonitored drop-offs, such as 24% at one Food Vans step.
My role
Sole product designer, from competitor analysis and testing to a new design system and developer hand-off process.
Key insight
With little developer time, we started with a low-risk service, Inflatable Pubs, that shares its flow with 90+ others. We could learn fast and scale what worked.
Outcome
Event requests rose 8.4% in the A/B test, and the new flow was rolled out to 94 more services.
01

Problem

Add to Event depends on organisers submitting event requests that suppliers then quote on. The request forms had been neglected: their conversion rate wasn't properly monitored, and our CRO expert found huge drop-offs at certain steps.

The goal was to improve form conversion across our range of services. More requests mean more quotes from suppliers, and more credits bought.

02

My role

As sole product designer I led the whole project: reviewing the existing forms, competitor analysis, concepts, prototyping and user testing, a dedicated design system, and a new way of handing designs to developers.

03

Process

Finding where people dropped off

We started with the steps that lost the most people, and used A/B tests on the existing Drupal forms to try new components and copy. I reviewed those steps on desktop, tablet and mobile. On the Food Vans service type step, for example:

  • Desktop users rarely scroll, so services at the bottom were probably never seen.
  • Icons or small images would be quicker to scan than large, sometimes low-quality stock photos.
  • The order of services wasn't alphabetical or obviously logical.
  • Long service names wrapped on mobile, using up vertical space.

Starting small to scale fast

Insight

Developer time was limited by a large backend migration, so we chose a low-risk service: Inflatable Pubs, which saw little traffic. It shares the same flow as 90+ of our 360 services, so anything we learned could be applied widely with confidence.

Learning from competitors

I analysed form experiences from direct competitors like Bark and Feast It, and from other industries. Three patterns stood out in the best forms:

  • They use basic elements well, with bigger selectable areas and custom icons.
  • They're technically solid: validation appears early and in context, and fields are pre-focused.
  • They add small encouragements, ask only for essentials, and collect extra details after submission.

Forms competitor analysis (PDF) ↓

A calmer, full-screen form

In Figma I stripped out cognitive load, such as "2000+ suppliers ready to quote" copy, and replaced the modal with a full-screen form on desktop. Icons made answers easier to recognise. I also reordered the questions so they flow naturally: date, time and location first, personal details last, once the value is clear.

Testing the new flow

Participants imagined booking an Inflatable Pub for a summer party. They told us:

  • The guide avatar felt personal, but it disappeared after the first step, which confused people.
  • Social sign-in would save them creating another account.
  • For guests and budget, they preferred picking from a visible list over a text field or a stepper.

Components and a new hand-off

To make designs easy for developers to find from Jira, I created a dedicated web components page in Figma. Each frame holds every state and edge case for one component, linked to its Jira ticket so the acceptance criteria were clear. Developers pinned questions directly on components, which brought design and development much closer.

A lightweight, embeddable form

Instead of a Drupal modal, the new forms run as a Stencil.js app on top of the site. That meant:

  • The form can be embedded anywhere, including the Drupal marketplace and the Webflow marketing site, replacing slow iframes that couldn't be A/B tested.
  • Progress is saved, so people can close the form and pick up where they left off.
  • We can A/B test bigger changes, like adding, removing and reordering steps.
  • Related questions can be grouped and swapped in and out freely.

A small design system of its own

A separate design system gave me full control over foundations, without fighting outdated legacy components, and let me refresh a UI that hadn't changed in four years. Components were built with variants for viewport, state and behaviour, so new ones are quick to add.

04

Outcome

+8.4%event requests submitted in the Inflatable Pubs A/B test
95services running the new form flow

After the successful test, the new flow was expanded to 94 more services. We kept watching Hotjar sessions for drop-offs, with a more complex service, Food Vans, and social sign-in lined up next.

The key deliverable was scalable technology: a solid design system and a lightweight Stencil.js stack that let the business improve its forms much faster.
