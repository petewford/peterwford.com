Add to Event · Organiser Dashboard

A one-stop shop to drive organiser repeat usage

Role
Sole product designer
Timeline
2022 – 2024
Disciplines
Research, UX, UI, prototyping, user testing
Problem
Only 21% of event organisers came back after their first request, and their experience hadn't been updated in over four years.
My role
Sole product designer, leading research, UX, UI, prototyping and user testing end to end.
Key insight
Most organisers find suppliers through word of mouth, but 42% prefer to find new ones. The inbox had to become a one-stop shop for planning, not just a list of quotes.
Outcome
A mobile-first organiser dashboard, tested on desktop and mobile prototypes and built into the testing app ahead of a closed beta.
01

Problem

Most organisers found Add to Event organically, submitted a request for one service, received quotes and left. Only 21% of them ever used the platform again, and the organiser experience hadn't been touched in over four years.

The goal was to increase repeat usage with a rebuilt organiser platform that encouraged organisers to come back and submit more service requests.

02

My role

As the sole product designer at Add to Event, I led every part of the design process, from the survey and UX audit through to prototypes, user testing and hand-off to developers working in two-week sprints.

03

Process

What organisers told us

A survey I sent to organisers showed we were working against the market: 72% usually found suppliers through word-of-mouth referrals, and 41% found social media useful for finding and contacting them. Neither needs a marketplace.

Insight

The same survey showed 42% of organisers prefer to find new suppliers for their events. That reframed the brief: the organiser inbox could become a one-stop shop for managing requests, comparing quotes and chatting with suppliers, while showing off the thousands of suppliers on the platform.

Auditing the old experience

A UX audit of the existing organiser experience surfaced five problems:

  • It was uninspiring. Events are celebrations, but nothing in the product carried that excitement.
  • It wasn't sticky. Organisers could review quotes, chat and accept or decline, but there were no benefits, social proof or upsells to bring them back.
  • Navigation differed between mobile and desktop. Getting back to the inbox on desktop was hard, and mobile relied on a makeshift switch between profile and chat.
  • Request statuses were unclear and buried in bloated text.
  • Text contrast and sizes failed WCAG AA across the board.

Mobile-first foundations, split into epics

Because the audit pointed at foundational problems I already knew how to solve, I went straight into Figma, starting with mobile navigation and hierarchy. I split the work into epics that matched how the team would build it, so each epic had its own focused prototype for testing and was easy for developers to break down.

Status tags instead of walls of text

I replaced the text-heavy request overview with a set of status tags mapped to backend events, such as "Awaiting approval" after a request is submitted. Each tag came with a short explanation of what's happening, plus a progress checker that updates as the organiser receives and accepts quotes.

One navigation pattern for every screen

Instead of custom navigation for one viewport that gets hidden on others, organisers got a single menu: a hamburger on mobile that becomes a permanent left-hand sidebar on desktop. It frees up space on small screens and leaves room for new sections later.

Flipping the accept/decline flow

To accept or decline a quote, organisers had to fill in a long, mandatory form explaining why, and who they chose instead. Few bothered, and suppliers often complained that organisers never replied. I flipped the flow: accept or decline first, then optionally say why. After each decision, the flow moves on to the organiser's other open quotes.

Testing the prototypes

I tested the desktop and mobile prototypes with eight participants each, using set tasks to measure completion. Three findings stood out:

  • While waiting for approval or quotes, people wanted an estimate of how long it would take.
  • Clicking through to reach a specific quote chat felt repetitive. A messages or notifications area would cut the number of taps.
  • An upcoming bookings area, for chatting with suppliers already booked, would help for the same reason.

Participants also responded well to the imagery. Contextual photos made requests easy to tell apart and brought the experience to life after the old greyscale design.

04

Outcome

The new organiser experience was built into the testing app, with a closed beta and Hotjar recordings planned as the next step to gather feedback and keep iterating.

The biggest lesson: you don't need to reinvent the wheel. The UX audit plus research into dashboard best practice gave me confidence to move fast, which mattered for stakeholders and let developers build iteratively.

What came next

The next big iteration groups service requests under one event, so organisers can request several services without repeating the same details, and opens the door to recommended services, venues and insurance. A notifications centre for status changes, supplier messages and reminders was the short-term priority, tied into email and SMS.
